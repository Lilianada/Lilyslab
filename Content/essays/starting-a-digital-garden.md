I, like many others in that community of [[indie web]] owners and [[webmasters]], decided that I wanted a digital-garden ecosystem: one that existed for itself, without the need for likes, retweets or followers. I wanted people to discover my digital garden not because an "algorithm" pushed my "content" to them, but because they were exploring the web and [[serendipitously]] encountered Lilyslab.

I built this website because I wanted a place where I could nurture my ideas, writings, projects, and research work and have them accumulate over time. I also wanted to do it in public as a way of meeting like-minded individuals. I am constantly planting seeds (notes) that reflect my thinking and are meant to stay evergreen[^1].

As someone with a background in software engineering, I'd like to teach others how to build their own websites for the purpose of starting a digital garden. If you'd like to learn more, browse through my [resources page](/resources).

A digital garden is simply a space (usually a personal website) where you keep a collection of [interlinked thoughts](https://www.mentalnodes.com), notes and ideas that you've written over time with the hopes of continually tending to them.

The aim of a digital garden is to help you draw connections between distinct ideas and to give you room to keep updating those ideas. As you grow, your garden grows with you. No work is truly finished in a digital garden.

### What a digital garden is not

- A digital garden is not a blog.

- A digital garden is not a collection of finished work.

- A digital garden is not meant for a large audience.

- A digital garden isn't arranged chronologically.

- A digital garden is not controlled by algorithms or chasing metrics.

### What a digital garden is

- A digital garden is a collection of interlinked notes that accumulate over time.

- A digital garden is a public space where you openly plant, tend to and grow seedlings of ideas into budding plants that would someday become evergreen.

- It is a space for playful and personal experimentation.

- It is used as a learning tool. You plant a seed, link it to other seeds and tend to it until it grows.

- A digital garden says, "Come as you are." A blog says, "Come clean and perfect, be without sin or iniquity."

### How to start a digital garden

To start your own digital garden, you need to have a personal website that you built from scratch or one hosted on platforms like [[Bear blog]], [[Micro blog]], WordPress or [Jekyll](https://indieweb.org/Jekyll). [Maxime](https://maximevaillancourt.com) even wrote a blog post on how to [set up your own digital garden with Jekyll](https://maximevaillancourt.com/blog/setting-up-your-own-digital-garden-with-jekyll). Knowing how to code is not a prerequisite for owning a personal website or starting a digital garden, but it is a good skill to hone. If you're really not interested in having anything to do with code or websites, platforms like Obsidian and Notion are a good alternative. You can still make your work public with either platform.

Once you have somewhere to publish, what should the setup of your website be, and what should you write about? I believe that the setup of your website depends solely on the content you want to put out. You can start lean and expand as you go. As for content, you should write only about things that interest you.

### How I tend my garden

I recently started releasing myself from the shackles of long essays. While I want to be knowledgeable and articulate, it helps me more to discuss a single idea in a note. I was initially using my notes as a dumping ground for only small thoughts and unfinished essays. Only recently did I realize that I could also use them to discuss ideas that help connect my writings better, building me an actual collection of interlinked notes.

These notes are connected by content and can be browsed by topic. Each note is supposed to link to one or more notes and so on. The more links between notes, the more garden-like it feels. A perfect example of a gardener who does this is [Mental Nodes](https://www.mentalnodes.com/). She uses one note for one idea. That way she's able to build a large collection of interlinked notes. This is not to say that you cannot have a list of all your long writings or notes, because I do have lists.

Remember that the goal of a digital garden is not to garner accolades from an audience the way most social networking platforms operate. So if no one visits your garden, remember that the garden is for you first and everyone else comes second. But if you need feedback from people or you want to meet like-minded people, then try joining [webrings](/webrings) you have an interest in. Sign people's guestbooks (you're welcome to sign [mine](/guestbook)), and be sure to ask questions of people you might want to learn from or interact with. You can also [ask me anything](/ask-me-anything).



[^1]: An evergreen note is one that's meant to stay useful over time. Instead of publishing it once and forgetting about it, you keep revisiting, revising and expanding it for as long as you keep thinking about the idea.
